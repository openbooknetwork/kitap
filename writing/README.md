# Writing

The English of a story is the source of every translation. These rules keep its sentences to one reading and its
names to the forms that maps and schoolbooks in every language use, so a reader of any translation meets the story
as if it were written in their language. The stories are written for readers of secondary school age and older. The
English still reads as ordinary prose. A content project's `AGENTS.md` adds its own rules, such as paragraph length
and the links a text makes.

## English

1. Put the main clause first, with at most one phrase or clause before it.
   "Having lost the port that had fed the city for a century, the merchants, many of whom had never left Izmir,
   began to sell what little they owned" becomes "After the city lost the port that had fed it for a century, the
   merchants began to sell what little they owned. Many of them had never left Izmir."
2. An opening phrase describes the subject of its sentence. "Walking to the harbour, the ships came into view"
   becomes "Walking to the harbour, she saw the ships."
3. Use a connective with one meaning: `because` for a cause, `when`, `after` or `while` for time, `although` or `but`
   for a contrast. `since` and `as` each carry both a time and a cause, so "As the Greeks advanced, the line broke"
   becomes "When the Greeks advanced, the line broke" or "Because the Greeks advanced, the line broke."
4. A pronoun points to one noun. When two nouns fit, name the one meant. "The officials told the merchants they
   would leave" becomes "The officials ordered the merchants to leave."
5. Keep the small words that show structure: `that` after a verb of saying and before a relative clause, and the
   articles. "the archive they burned" becomes "the archive that they burned."
6. Spell out a stack of nouns. "port tax revenue records" becomes "records of the revenue from port taxes."
7. Name the year when the text moves in time and the page has not named it yet. A reader can open any page first. "That
   spring the army marched east" becomes "In spring 1921 the army marched east." Dates read day, month, year: 26 August
   1921. A year before the Common Era takes BC after it: 216 BC. An approximate year takes about: about 1450.
8. Say plainly what an idiom, a pun or a double negative means. "not uncommon" becomes "common", "turned a blind
   eye" becomes "ignored", "the tide turned" becomes what changed.
9. A person, place, state or structure has one name at a time through a story: the name its marker, battle card or
   part uses. Another name may follow in brackets, once per page or label.
10. A place takes the English name it has today, the one on maps: Thessaloniki, Gyumri, Cape Helles, Chunuk Bair,
    Anzac Cove. The name of the time follows in brackets where it differs: Gyumri (then Alexandropol). An event or
    a treaty keeps the name histories give it: the Treaty of Alexandropol, the Salonica front.
11. A person or a state takes the name of the time: Mustafa Kemal until the surname law of 1934 and Atatürk after
    it, the Republic of Turkey in 1923 and Türkiye today. The later name may follow in brackets: İsmet Pasha (later
    İnönü). A story's title and summary name their subject as readers know it today.
12. Measures are metric. A figure taken from miles, yards or feet keeps the precision of the original: under
    two miles becomes under 3 km, about 800 yards becomes about 700 m.

Long sentences that continue after the main clause read and translate well. So do the passive, when the actor is
unknown or does not matter, metaphors that make sense word for word, and synonyms for ordinary nouns.

Labels follow the same rules: titles, captions, marker names and the labels of a battle plan. A label is a noun
phrase or one short sentence.

## Translating

1. Translate a paragraph at a time. Split, join and reorder its sentences as the language reads best, and keep every
   fact, date, number, name and hedge such as "about" or "probably".
2. Keep the markup: each `@image <id>` line, each link target `(@id)` and the paragraph breaks. A link covers the name
   with the endings and suffixes the language attaches to it. An article, a preposition or a particle written as a
   separate word stays outside the link, unless it is part of the name.
3. A name takes the form the language's own maps and schoolbooks use: Seddülbahir in Turkish for Cape Helles, Салоники
   in Russian for Thessaloniki. An event takes the name the language's own histories give it. A name with no such form
   is translated where it describes the place, as Lone Pine and Baby 700 do, and transliterated by the language's usual
   system otherwise. A transliteration that spells a word with another meaning, rude or grim, takes another spelling.
   The story's catalogue holds each form, so every page and the picture agree. When the language has one name for both
   forms of a bracketed name, the bracket goes: "Thessaloniki (then Salonica)" is Selanik in Turkish. The terms of a
   subject, such as a battle's or an organ's, stay the same through a story.
4. The tense follows the source. A story told in the present stays in the present.
5. Numbers, dates, times of day and units follow the language's own conventions. A number stays on one line with its
   unit or its suffix: a space between them is a no break space (U+00A0, or the narrow U+202F where the language's file
   says so), and a hyphen between them is a no break hyphen (U+2011).
6. Punctuation follows the language's own rules, its dashes, semicolons and name separators included.

7. Wording about a disputed event, border or territory stays as neutral as the source. Where the language's official
   or partisan term takes a side, the term that names both sides replaces it.

A file per language in this folder, such as [tr.md](tr.md), holds that language's conventions: how the text and the
interface address the reader, which past tense tells history, and how it writes years before the Common Era and a
ruler's number.
